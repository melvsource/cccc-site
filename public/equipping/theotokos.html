Theology for the Church

What's in a Name?
Theotokos and the Question Behind the Question

An ancient debate about Mary turns out to be entirely about Jesus.

CCCC  ·  Christology Series

Back to Equipping

In AD 431, a church council met in the city of Ephesus over a dispute that, on the surface, seemed to be about what to call Mary. One side wanted to call her Theotokos — a Greek word meaning "God-bearer" or "Mother of God." The other side preferred Christotokos — "Christ-bearer." Simple enough, right?

Not quite. The two words were symptoms of two very different understandings of who Jesus is.

The Man Who Started It

Nestorius was the Patriarch of Constantinople — one of the most important church leaders of his day. He was not a bad man, and he wasn't trying to undermine the faith. He had a reasonable concern: the title "Mother of God" sounded philosophically strange. How can an eternal, infinite God have a mother? He wanted to protect the majesty of God from what felt like a clumsy phrase.

So he proposed Christotokos — "Bearer of Christ." More careful. More precise. Mary gave birth to Jesus the Messiah, not to the eternal God himself.

It sounded sensible. But a theologian named Cyril of Alexandria saw the problem immediately.

The Real Issue

Cyril's argument was this: the moment you say Mary gave birth to Christ but not to the Son of God, you've quietly split Jesus into two separate persons — a human one and a divine one, loosely joined together like a hand inside a glove.

The debate was never really about Mary.
It was about whether Jesus is truly one person — or two. — The Council of Ephesus, AD 431

If Jesus is two persons, then the one who was born, who suffered, and who died on the cross was the human person — and the divine Son of God was somehow standing apart from all of it. Which means the cross doesn't carry the weight we need it to carry.

But if Jesus is one person — fully God and fully human, inseparably united — then to give birth to him is to give birth to God incarnate. Theotokos protects that truth. It keeps Jesus whole.

So Was Christotokos Wrong?

Here's where it gets nuanced — and it's worth getting right.

The Council of Ephesus did not ban the word Christotokos. The word itself isn't false. Mary did bear Christ. The problem was what Nestorius meant by preferring it — the theology hiding underneath his word choice. He preferred it because, in his mind, it kept the divine and human in Jesus safely separated.

The Ruling in Plain Terms

In the hands of someone who believes Jesus is one unified person, both Theotokos and Christotokos can be used faithfully together. What the Council condemned was the theology behind Nestorius's preference — not the word itself.

Think of it this way: a title can be used as a window or as a wall. Nestorius was using Christotokos as a wall — to keep the divine and human in Christ at arm's length. The Council insisted that no wall belongs there.

Why It Still Matters

This isn't ancient trivia. The question of whether Jesus is truly, completely one person — God and man, permanently united — sits at the very heart of the gospel.

It means the one who held the nails was the same one who spoke the universe into existence. It means the death on Calvary was not merely a human tragedy but a divine act of rescue. It means when we pray, we are not reaching for a distant God who watched the cross from afar — we are speaking to the one who was in it.

The church in Ephesus didn't just defend a title for Mary. They defended the wholeness of Jesus — and with it, the wholeness of the hope we carry.
