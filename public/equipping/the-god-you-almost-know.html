The God You Almost Know

Acts 17:16–34 — Paul in Athens

Proclamation in public 7 Principles · Study questions included
Back to Equipping
I

The scene

Athens was not a hostile crowd — it was an indifferent one, which in some ways is harder. The city was full of altars, shrines, and philosophical schools. It was a place where ideas competed openly and where a new voice could earn a hearing if it was interesting enough.

Paul arrived not as a visiting lecturer but as a tentmaker passing through. What he saw in the marketplace stirred something deep in him — the Greek word used is paroxysm, a sharp provocation of spirit. But what is remarkable is what he did with that feeling: he did not preach in anger. He went into the marketplace and started talking to whoever would listen.

It worked. He was curious enough that some philosophers brought him to the Areopagus — the formal public platform for debating new ideas — and asked him to explain himself. A man who started in a market stall ended up on the city's main stage.

II

What Paul did

He let grief become compassion. The provocation Paul felt at the idolatry around him did not come out as accusation. Something converted it — likely the memory of his own encounter with grace — and what left his mouth was not correction but an invitation.

He acknowledged what he saw in them. His opening line was not a rebuke. He observed that the Athenians were clearly people who took the question of God seriously. That single acknowledgment raised the room's attention before he said anything theological.

"Men of Athens, I perceive that in every way you are very religious. For as I passed along and observed the objects of your worship, I found also an altar with this inscription: 'To the unknown god.'" Acts 17:22–23

He found the open door. Rather than attacking their idols, Paul pointed to a gap they had already admitted — an altar to a god they could not name. He used their own acknowledgment of the unknown as his entry point. He was not vague about where he was headed: he named his subject immediately.

He reasoned from Scripture without quoting it directly. Paul's speech is soaked in Genesis. The God who "made the world and everything in it," who "does not live in temples made by hands," who "gives to all mankind life and breath" — this is the Creator of Genesis 1 and 2, brought forward into Athenian vocabulary. Paul was not inventing a new theology for his audience; he was translating the Scripture they had never read into categories they already understood.

He reasoned toward an undeniable conclusion. From the premise that God made everything and needs nothing from creation, Paul drew the logical consequence: creatures are not self-sufficient. If creatures depend on a Creator for life and breath and everything, the right movement is back toward that Creator — for meaning, identity, and purpose.

He honored what was true in their world. Quoting the philosopher Aratus — "For we are indeed his offspring" — Paul did not destroy the Athenians' existing framework. He found the true thing buried inside it and held it up to the light. This was not compromise. It was an act of intellectual generosity that completed rather than demolished.

He landed at repentance, Christ, and the resurrection. After the entire arc — empathy, the unknown God, creation, dependence, seeking — Paul brought it home: God commands all people everywhere to repent, because a day of judgment has been fixed. And the proof he offered was the resurrection of Jesus — which happened to be the very thing that had sparked the Athenians' curiosity in the first place.

III

The principles

Principle 1The messenger's internal grief must become outward compassion before the mouth opens. How you feel about sin is not your message — how God feels about people is.
Principle 2The listener must feel seen before they can truly hear. Strategic empathy is not flattery — it is the honest acknowledgment of what a person is already reaching for.
Principle 3Effective proclamation finds the open door the listener doesn't know they've left open. Look for the gap, the question, the unnamed longing — and name it for them.
Principle 4The Word drives the proclamation even when it is not quoted by chapter and verse. Scripture is the spine, not the decoration.
Principle 5The Gospel can bear the weight of logical scrutiny. Do not be afraid to reason with your listener. A well-ordered argument is not a lack of faith — it is love taking the listener seriously.
Principle 6Look for what is true in the listener's world before pointing to what is missing. Honor the real thing before correcting the broken one.
Principle 7End at the cross and the resurrection. Everything before it is preparation, not destination.

Paul's method at a glance — Engaged Proclamation

1Let grief become compassion — the right interior posture before you speak
2See the person before speaking to them — strategic empathy
3Find the open door — the bridge point already present in their world
4Anchor in Scripture — even when invisible, the Word is the spine
5Reason toward the conclusion — from premise to undeniable implication
6Honor what is true in their world — cultural empathy, not cultural capitulation
7Call to repentance through Christ — the non-negotiable landing point
IV & V

Reflect and practice

Reflect

Think of someone in your life who seems spiritually indifferent or unreachable. What is the "altar to an unknown god" in their life — the gap or longing they've already admitted, even without realizing it? How might that become a door?

Practice this week

Have one conversation where you ask before you tell. Ask someone what they think gives life meaning, or what they are afraid of losing. Listen fully. Notice what door they leave open — and write it down afterward.
